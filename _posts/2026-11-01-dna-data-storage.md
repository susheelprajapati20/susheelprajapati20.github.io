# What If We Could Store the World's Data in DNA?

*Can a tiny amount of DNA store information that would otherwise require enormous amounts of conventional storage? And could this idea help us reduce electronic waste?*

Think about all the information we create every day.

YouTube videos, Instagram posts, university assignments, office documents, scientific research, photographs, websites, and millions of other digital files.

Where will we store all this information in the future?

Today, we depend on hard drives, SSDs, memory cards, servers, and huge data centres. These technologies are essential, but they also require physical materials, electricity, maintenance, and eventual replacement.

While exploring this problem, I came across an interesting idea: **what if we could store digital information in DNA—the same molecule that carries genetic information in living organisms?**

It sounds like science fiction, but researchers have already demonstrated that digital files can be stored in synthetic DNA and recovered later.

Let's understand how it works, how much information DNA could theoretically hold, and whether it could help us build a more sustainable digital future.

## 1. First, what is DNA?

DNA stands for *deoxyribonucleic acid*. It carries genetic instructions in almost all living organisms.

DNA uses four chemical building blocks, commonly represented by four letters:

- **A** — Adenine
- **T** — Thymine
- **G** — Guanine
- **C** — Cytosine

You can think of these letters as a biological alphabet.

Just as computers use 0s and 1s to represent information, researchers can use combinations of A, T, G, and C to represent digital data.

For example, a computer file can be converted into a sequence of DNA letters. Later, that sequence can be read and converted back into the original file.

This does not mean that DNA naturally understands YouTube videos or PDF documents. Scientists design a process that translates digital information into molecular sequences and back again.

**The exciting part is that the same type of molecule that stores genetic information can also be used to store information created by humans.**

## 2. How much information can DNA store?

This is where the idea becomes particularly interesting.

Some sources quote a theoretical estimate of about **455 exabytes per gram** based on idealized encoding assumptions. This is not a measured capacity for a complete, reliable storage system; real systems need extra material and data for encoding, addressing, error correction, and recovery. For broader background, see this review of molecular data storage ([Ceze, Nivala, and Strauss, 2019](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41576-019-0125-3)).

An exabyte is an enormous unit of digital storage:

- 1 terabyte (TB) = 1,000 gigabytes (GB)
- 1 petabyte (PB) = 1,000 TB
- 1 exabyte (EB) = 1,000 PB

The 455 EB figure is a theoretical estimate, not a guarantee of how much data a complete, reliable DNA storage system can store.

In a 2020 study published in *Nature Communications*, researchers demonstrated reliable digital file recovery using synthetic DNA and reported an effective density of approximately **17 exabytes per gram** under their particular experimental conditions.

Why is this number lower than the theoretical estimate? A practical system needs extra information to identify DNA fragments, handle errors, and recover files reliably.

The two figures describe different assumptions, so they should not be treated as directly equivalent commercial capacities.

### Let's calculate something ourselves

Suppose we want to represent **one petabyte of data** in DNA.

One petabyte equals 0.001 exabytes.

Using the theoretical estimate: `0.001 EB / (455 EB/g) ≈ 0.00000220 g`, or about **2.2 micrograms**.

Using the experimental effective density of 17 EB/g: `0.001 EB / (17 EB/g) ≈ 0.0000588 g`, or about **58.8 micrograms**.

These are calculations based on published density estimates. They do not represent the total size of a working storage system, which would also require equipment, packaging, preservation, and data-recovery processes.

Still, the numbers show why researchers are interested in DNA as a storage medium.

## 3. How can a video or document be stored in DNA?

Imagine that a university wants to preserve an old research video for the next 100 years.

A DNA storage system would follow a process similar to this:

**Step 1: Convert the file into digital data.**

The video is represented as a sequence of binary digits, just as it is in conventional computer storage.

**Step 2: Convert the data into DNA sequences.**

Specialized software translates the binary information into sequences made from A, T, G, and C.

**Step 3: Create the DNA.**

Specialized laboratory equipment produces synthetic DNA molecules matching those sequences.

**Step 4: Preserve the DNA.**

The molecules are stored using suitable preservation methods to protect them from damage.

**Step 5: Read the information when needed.**

A sequencing process reads the DNA letters. Software then reconstructs the original digital file.

The recovered file can be opened on a computer just like an ordinary video or document.

![Diagram showing DNA data retrieval through file selection, sequencing, clustering, error correction, and reconstruction](/images/dna-storage-process.png)

*The diagram illustrates how sequencing and error correction help reconstruct data from DNA fragments.*

Of course, this process is much more complicated than copying a file to a USB drive. DNA synthesis and sequencing require specialized equipment and procedures.

But the underlying idea is straightforward: **translate digital information into DNA, preserve it, and translate it back when needed.**

## 4. Have scientists actually done this?

Yes. DNA data storage is an active research field, and researchers have demonstrated several important parts of the process.

### Experiment 1: Recovering files from synthetic DNA

In 2020, researchers published a paper titled *Probing the physical limits of reliable DNA data retrieval* in *Nature Communications*.

They demonstrated that digital files could be recovered from a complex pool of synthetic DNA. Their approach also explored how to retrieve selected information without reading every DNA sequence in the collection.

The study reported an effective storage density of approximately 17 EB/g under its experimental conditions.

**What does this prove?**

It shows that DNA can be used to store and recover digital information in a laboratory.

It does not prove that we can already store the world's entire digital archive in DNA at an affordable price.

Research paper: [Nature Communications (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-020-14319-8)

### Experiment 2: Exploring other DNA storage approaches

Researchers are also investigating ways to make DNA storage easier to produce. A 2024 study reported gel-based electrochemical DNA synthesis for quasi-solid-state data storage. This is a research approach to DNA synthesis, not a demonstration of a mature commercial archive.

Research paper: [Feng and colleagues, *Chemical Engineering Journal* (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cej.2024.150485)

These experiments demonstrate genuine scientific progress, but DNA storage still faces significant challenges before it can become a practical option for large commercial archives.

## 5. Could DNA store millions of YouTube videos and university records?

In principle, digital video files, research papers, images, documents, and datasets can all be represented as digital information and encoded into DNA.

Imagine a future archival system designed to preserve:

- Educational videos and documentaries
- University research papers and historical records
- Scientific datasets and experimental results
- Public government documents
- Old websites and cultural collections
- Business records that must be preserved for many years

Instead of keeping every archival copy on conventional storage devices, organizations might eventually store selected collections in DNA.

However, not every file should be stored this way.

Consider a university's online student portal. Students need to access their records quickly, update information, and register for courses. Conventional computers and storage systems are better suited to this work.

An old research collection that is rarely accessed is a different situation. It may be a better candidate for long-term archival storage.

This suggests a practical approach:

| Type of information | Suitable approach |
|---|---|
| Applications and frequently used files | SSDs and conventional storage |
| Large collections accessed regularly | Hard drives and other established storage systems |
| Backups and long-term archives | Magnetic tape and other archival technologies |
| Selected archives preserved for decades | DNA could become an additional option |

DNA would not replace all computers or hard drives. It could become another tool for preserving information that does not need to be accessed every day.

## 6. Could this help reduce electronic waste?

This is one of the most important questions behind the idea.

According to the United Nations' *Global E-waste Monitor 2024*, the world generated **62 million tonnes of electronic waste in 2022**.

Only **22.3%** of that waste was documented as formally collected and recycled.

The report projected that global e-waste could reach 82 million tonnes by 2030.

These figures cover electronic waste from many kinds of electrical and electronic equipment—not just hard drives or data centres.

Source: [Global E-waste Monitor 2024](https://ewastemonitor.info/the-global-e-waste-monitor-2024/)

Why does this matter for DNA storage?

Conventional storage relies on physical devices and supporting infrastructure. If DNA eventually replaces some conventional hardware used for long-term archives, it could reduce the number of devices required for those particular applications.

Potential benefits might include:

- Less physical space required for some archives
- Fewer conventional storage devices needed for selected workloads
- Less frequent replacement of archival media
- Potential reductions in energy and cooling requirements
- Reduced demand for some materials used in storage hardware

But there is an important limitation.

**DNA storage does not automatically eliminate electronic waste or the need for metals.**

Scientists still need laboratory equipment, computers, sequencing systems, chemicals, and preservation materials. These also have environmental costs.

The real benefit depends on whether the complete DNA storage system uses fewer resources than the conventional system it replaces.

Researchers must compare the full process, including DNA production, preservation, retrieval, equipment manufacturing, and disposal.

## 7. Why aren't we storing everything in DNA already?

If DNA is so compact, why don't we use it for everything?

There are several reasons.

### It can be expensive to write data

Creating synthetic DNA requires specialized processes and equipment. Producing enormous quantities of DNA at an affordable cost remains a major challenge.

### Reading data is not as simple as opening a file

With a conventional drive, a computer can retrieve a file quickly. Recovering information from DNA generally requires laboratory preparation and sequencing.

This makes DNA unsuitable for many applications that need immediate access.

### DNA can be damaged

DNA molecules can be affected by environmental conditions and chemical damage. A reliable archive needs suitable preservation methods and ways to detect and correct errors.

### Updating information is difficult

We regularly edit documents, update databases, and delete files. DNA storage is better suited to information that remains unchanged for long periods than to data that is constantly rewritten.

### We need to measure the complete environmental cost

DNA may take up very little physical space, but producing and reading it also requires resources. Researchers need to establish whether the complete system offers environmental benefits over conventional storage.

A 2020 Microsoft Research study investigated the potential environmental impact of DNA-based archival storage compared with hard drives and magnetic tape. It found promising results in the scenarios modelled, but the system was hypothetical rather than a fully deployed commercial data centre.

Research: [Microsoft Research — Greener Data Storage Using Synthetic DNA](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/architecting-datacenters-for-sustainability-greener-data-storage-using-synthetic-dna/)

## 8. Can students work on this idea today?

Yes. Even without a biotechnology laboratory, students can investigate the digital side of DNA storage.

A useful project would be a simple Python application called **BioArchive**.

The application could take a small text file or image and convert its binary data into a sequence of DNA-like letters.

It could then divide the sequence into smaller fragments, simulate errors, reconstruct the original file, and check whether the recovered file matches the original.

A student team could investigate questions such as:

- How much extra data is needed to improve reliability?
- What happens when some DNA fragments are missing?
- How do different fragment sizes affect recovery?
- Can error-correction methods recover files after simulated damage?
- How does the theoretical DNA mass change with archive size?

Python could handle the file conversion, while SHA-256 checksums could verify whether the reconstructed file is identical to the original.

This would be a software simulation, not a physical DNA storage experiment. Nevertheless, it would help students understand the mathematics, coding, and reliability challenges behind the technology.

## 9. What might the future look like?

I do not think DNA will simply replace every hard drive, SSD, or data centre.

A more realistic possibility is that different technologies will serve different purposes.

Conventional storage will remain important for applications that need speed and frequent updates. Magnetic tape and hard drives will continue to serve many archival and backup needs.

DNA could become an additional option for selected collections that must be preserved for decades or longer.

Imagine a university preserving generations of research papers, a scientific institution archiving historical observations, or a cultural organization protecting important digital collections.

If DNA storage becomes sufficiently reliable, affordable, and environmentally beneficial, it could help preserve such information in a very compact form.

But that future depends on solving real engineering problems—not simply on DNA's impressive theoretical capacity.

## Conclusion: Can we preserve more knowledge with fewer resources?

The idea of storing digital information in DNA connects biology, computer science, engineering, and environmental sustainability.

Researchers have already demonstrated that digital files can be encoded into synthetic DNA and recovered. The theoretical storage density is extraordinary, and studies have explored how this technology might support long-term archives.

At the same time, high costs, slow retrieval, reliability, and environmental trade-offs remain important challenges.

The goal should not be to declare DNA the replacement for all existing storage technologies. It should be to discover where DNA can genuinely do a better job.

For me, the most interesting question is this:

**As humanity continues to generate more and more information, can we find a way to preserve that knowledge for future generations while using fewer physical resources?**

DNA storage is one possible answer worth investigating.

What do you think? Could molecular storage become an important part of our digital future, or will conventional storage technologies remain the better choice?
